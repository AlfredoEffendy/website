When Yield Curves Invert Together:
Currency Crash Risk∗
Alfredo Effendy†
University of Washington

Abstract
A yield-curve inversion is a well-documented leading indicator of domestic slowdown,
but a single inversion does not reveal whether the expected slowdown is country-specific
or global. This distinction, however, decisively predicts the asset market response to
the impending slowdown. In a portfolio of currencies, such as the carry trade, currency-
specific risk might be diversified, while systematic risk is affecting every risky currency.
I show that a signal constructed from clustered inversions predicts crashes in high-
interest and high-beta currencies, and the carry trade. This result plays a role in giving
an early warning to avoid the well-known carry trade crashes. Further conditioning
on the slope shape of global yield curves refines the signal to distinguish between
inversions driven by slowing growth expectations versus inflation-fighting monetary
policies. The latter comes with a higher interest rates environment, which provides a
higher compensation for the carry trade crash risk.

Keywords: Inverted Yield Curve; Disaster Risk; Exchange Rate; Currency Carry Trade.

Introduction
The currency carry trade borrows in low-interest-rate currencies and invests in high-interest-
rate ones. It has been profitable on average because high-interest-rate currencies do not
depreciate enough to offset the interest rate differential (IRD), in violation of uncovered
interest parity (UIP) (Hansen and Hodrick, 1980; Fama, 1984; Engel, 2016). The modern
literature interprets this premium as compensation for depreciation risk and organizes cur-
rency returns into priced factors (Lustig and Verdelhan, 2007; Lustig et al., 2011; Menkhoff
et al., 2012). The premium accumulates gradually, but much of it can disappear during
currency crashes arising from unwinds of carry trade positions and funding spirals. These
crashes create negative return skewness (Brunnermeier et al., 2008), the carry trade has
exhibited across various financial market and monetary regimes in the past century (Ac-
cominotti et al., 2019). In disaster models, these losses are the realizations for which the
interest rate differential compensates investors in advance (Farhi and Gabaix, 2016; Chernov
et al., 2018). For a carry investor, the question that matters is whether an impending crash
is predictable.
Answering that question requires an indicator of when currency downside risk is elevated.
Government yield curves encode market expectations about economic activity in real time,
and an inversion has long been understood as a recession signal (Harvey, 1988; Estrella
and Hardouvelis, 1991; Campbell and Shiller, 1991; Estrella and Mishkin, 1998; Bauer and
Mertens, 2018). I find that a signal constructed from cross-country clusters of fresh yield
curve inversion predict currency crashes.
Although the signal is only active in about a
fifth of the sample, it predicts four of the give worst months in carry trade. The losses
are concentrated in high-interest-rate and high-beta currencies. Figure
1 illustrates the
relationship between the signal and carry trade crashes.
This finding suggests that the bond market leads the currency market, with yield curve
inversions providing an early warning of currency losses. Two features of the signal help

simplest way to detect a cluster is to count how many curves are inverted in a given month,
a measure that I call the naive count. This captures clustering, but treats fresh and stale
inversions alike, potentially diluting the signal. Therefore, it makes sense to specifically look
at clusters of fresh inversion.
There are several ways to account for freshness.
This paper focuses on an approach
that uses the information from the yield curve itself. A curve becomes active when it first
inverts and remains active until its slope has steepened for two consecutive months, whether
or not the slope has returned above zero. Steepening indicates that either a recovery is
underway or the market has begun to price in the inversion, while the two-month confirmation
follows the two-quarter recession conventions used in the United Kingdom, Canada, and
Australia. Requiring the confirmation also handles curves whose slope hovers around zero,
which might otherwise create a noisy alternating inversion state. An alternative approach
is to penalize older clusters by weighting the naive count by the inverse of the cluster’s age,
which Appendix B.2 reports
I construct the IYC signal, from the G10 government yield curves, with both distinctions
above built in. The baseline configuration for the yield curve slope is the ten-year yield
minus the two-year yield, and Appendix B.1 repeats the construction with the ten-year
minus the three-month yield. I use a simple threshold of two active inversions to separate
a country-specific inversion from a cluster, since two is the smallest count that rules out
a single-country event. The signal is on in 93 of the 458 sample months (∼20%), across
ten different episodes. Figure 1 shows how the freshness consideration separates the IYC
signal from the naive count, which stays active for more months, and Section 2 gives the
full construction of the signal.
Persistently high-interest-rate economies are often commodity exporters and small open
economies that are exposed to the global cycle (Ready et al., 2017; Della Corte et al., 2016).1

The carry trade inherits this exposure because it is long high-interest-rate, high-beta curren-
cies and short low-interest-rate, low-beta currencies. When the signal is active, a portfolio
that is long high-beta currencies and short low-beta currencies (beta HML) loses 9.24%
annualized in spot terms (8.29% including IRD), against a gain of 3.57% (5.29% including
IRD)when it is inactive. Similarly, the carry trade spot leg loses 8.99% annualized (3.67%
including IRD) against a gain of 2.64% (6.19% including IRD). By contrast, a currency
whose own curve is active while the signal is off does not depreciate on average, and months
with exactly one active inversion remain favorable for the carry trade, returning 10.09%
annualized.
The signal changes more than the average return. During these signal months, portfolio
skewness becomes more negative and returns deteriorate throughout the distribution, with
the largest changes in the left tail, accounting for much of the negative skewness of the
carry trade. Leading indicators also decline across G10 economies after the signal months,
including in the United States.
The currency and macroeconomic results both point to
a shared weakening in expected activity rather than the isolated domestic slowdown that
a single inversion may signal. In the language of the disaster-risk literature (Rietz, 1988;
Barro, 2006; Wachter, 2013), I interpret the IYC signal as an observable indicator of elevated
currency downside risk.
An inversion can arise in two distinct environments. Markets may expect rates to fall
because activity is weakening, or because a policy rate raised to fight inflation is expected
to normalize. A growth-scare inversion tends to occur with the IRD near its ordinary level,
leaving the subsequent depreciation poorly compensated.
An inflation-fighting inversion
occurs in a high-rate environment, in which the position collects unusually wide IRD. This
leads to an important question, the signal identifies a period of elevated risk, but it does not
say whether the IRD compensates enough of that risk to stay invested.
The shape of the full curve helps distinguish these cases. When the three-month yield
stands above the two-year yield and the two-year yield stands above the ten-year yield, the

short rate is the highest point on the curve. This shape is consistent with a policy rate
held high while the market expects its eventual normalization. I call this strictly downward-
sloping configuration the downcurve and measure it using the average G10 curve. In months
with this downcurve shape, the IRD covers most of the depreciation that follows, while
outside the downcurve it only covers a small fraction of the loss. One can think of the signal
as flagging an elevated crash risk, while the IRD and the downcurve indicate whether bearing
that risk is adequately compensated.2
The next question is how to apply the signal in real-time portfolio risk management. The
natural response is to unwind the position when the signal is active to avoid the crashes.
Doing so forgoes the IRD, which in downcurve months offsets most of the loss. I call the risk
of surrendering this compensation carry-premium risk, and the decision to remain invested
through it the carry overwrite. The investor can also hedge the tail with options (Jurek,
2014) or reverse into anti-carry (Caballero and Doyle, 2012) during these signal months.
One potential concern is that the signal only repackages the global yield curve, the U.S.
yield curve, or standard risk and volatility predictors such as the VIX, FX volatility, a
commodity index, and the NFCI. I therefore run a horse race regression against all those
measures, including each individual country’s inversion, the U.S. included and the results’
power survives or even strengthens in some cases.

Conditioning carry performance on observable states is not new. Clarida et al. (2009)
and Christiansen et al. (2011) allow the trade’s returns and risk exposures to vary with
volatility and liquidity regimes, while Barroso and Santa-Clara (2015) construct currency
portfolios from observable characteristics. Related work conditions on average interest-rate
levels (Lustig et al., 2014), macroeconomic inflation expectations (Andrews et al., 2024),
and commodity, volatility, and liquidity predictors (Bakshi and Panayotov, 2013). The new
element is the conditioning variable, a fresh cross-country cluster of government yield-curve
inversions that identifies a common state before the associated currency losses are realized,
and I am not aware of any prior currency work that uses overlapping fresh inversions across
countries in this way.

distribution, and the hedging application. Section 4 compares the signal with rival curve
measures and standard risk indicators. Section 5 extends the analysis to emerging markets.

2.3
The Risk Compensation Ratio and Hedging
The natural application of the signal is to unwind the carry portfolio, or to reverse it into
anti-carry, while the signal is on. An investor considering either would want to know the
opportunity cost of hedging, which is the income the position would stop collecting, and
how that cost compares with the crash risk the hedge avoids.
The benchmark for that
comparison is the Fama (1984) regression of appreciation on the lagged differential, whose
parity value is a slope of −1 and whose estimate sits near zero, the familiar puzzle. In most
months the differential is collected with no systematic depreciation offsetting it, so a hedge
is expensive on average, and the question is whether the signal marks the states in which
the ordering reverses. conditioning (3) on the signal makes the comparison explicit. The
expected return is the income leg Ct plus the expected spot move, and if the spot risk in the

2.5
Data
The main sample is monthly and runs from January 1988 to February 2026. The baseline
universe is the nine G10 currencies (AUD, NZD, CAD, NOK, SEK, GBP, EUR, CHF, and
JPY), and I use the DEM as a proxy for the EUR before 1999. For each currency I use
its exchange rate against the dollar, its one-month money-market rate, and its 3M, 2Y, and
10Y government bond yields. The currency betas are estimated on daily (monthly) exchange
rate and S&P 500 (MSCI World) returns, both of which are available 90 trading days (24
months) before the start of the sample.
For the EM extension I add the seven most liquid floating currencies, BRL, CLP, IDR,
INR, MXN, KRW, and ZAR. Due to data availability, their sample is shorter. The last
money-market rate to start is the Chilean, in January 1996, so the EM panel begins in
February 1996, while the alternative signal built on the EM curves themselves begins in
June 2003.
Among the other variables the NFCI, the CLI, and CPI inflation are available from
January 1988, while the VIX begins in October 1990. The CLI ends in November 2019 for
New Zealand and in November 2022 for Switzerland, Norway, and Sweden, and I extend
each with that country’s business or consumer confidence index, which affects few of the
signal months. All data are obtained from Bloomberg, Finaeon, central bank datasets, and
the Chicago Fed. Appendix A sets out the economic regimes the sample spans.

3.5
Portfolio Trading and Risk Management Application
The question a carry trader faces is what to do with the signal. Section 2.3 gives three
answers, unwinding the portfolio, reversing into anti-carry, and the downcurve overwrite,
which stays invested in the months the differential pays for the risk. Table 8 reports what
each does to the portfolio, as an overlay on the carry trade’s total returns.7 Figure 7 plots
the cumulative returns.
Unwinding on the signal raises the carry trade’s Sharpe ratio from 0.53 to 0.73 and cuts
its worst month from −11.61 to −7.27%, and the two overwrites raise it further, to 0.78 and
0.87, by staying invested through the months the differential pays for. The skewness moves
with it, from −0.68 always invested to −0.34 under the unwind and to −0.01 under the
anti-carry overwrite, so the negative skewness that defines the carry trade in the literature
(Brunnermeier et al., 2008) is largely removed. The gain is economically meaningful on both
counts, though it is marginal in statistical terms for the carry portfolio and clearer for the
beta HML portfolio.

the same core portfolio in every state (Appendix B.6). Over the full sample the regime does

4.1
Common versus Idiosyncratic, and versus the U.S. Curve
A natural concern about the signal is that it may be no more than a proxy for the global slope
or for the U.S. curve, which is often deemed a global proxy. The concern is fair, because the
signal aggregates those same slopes in the first place. It differs from both by construction,
in the way Section 2.2 sets out, and Table 9 confirms that the difference matters.
Racing the signal against the level of the global average slope and its one-month change,
the U.S. inversion, and the level and one-month change of the U.S. slope leaves none of
the five rivals statistically significant. Adding them, in fact, strengthens the signal’s own
32


===== PAGE 34 =====
coefficient in every leg.
The third candidate is a currency’s own curve, which the domestic interpretation of
Section 2.2 would make the natural conditioning variable, and which matters here because
bilateral exchange rates load heavily on common components (Verdelhan, 2018). The same
conclusion holds currency by currency against the dollar, in Table B.10 of the appendix. A
currency whose own signal is active while the IYC signal is off does not depreciate, at a
pooled +2.90%/yr and insignificant, and when currencies do depreciate on the IYC signal
the losses fall on the high-beta names, not on the countries whose curves are in the count.

4.2
Global Risk Gauges and Currency Factors
Another way to test the signal’s robustness is to put it against the predictors the literature
already uses. One might argue that it is only a proxy for the VIX, for funding stress, or for
the credit cycle. Bakshi and Panayotov (2013) also time the carry trade with a commodity
return, an average currency volatility, and a funding-liquidity measure. To test the signal
against all of these, I run a ladder of nested specifications, where each column adds a new
block of controls and keeps the ones before it. I order the blocks by how far back their series

6
Conclusion
This paper contributes to the yield curve and the international asset pricing literature by
showing that clusters of fresh G10 yield curve inversions identify periods of elevated currency
downside risk. Freshness separates inversions that still carry timing information from those
that have gone stale. The depreciation falls on high-beta currencies, which also tend to have
high interest rates, so the carry trade inherits this exposure through its long leg. The signal
is active in about 20% of the sample and captures four of the five worst months of the carry
trade. The predictive power remains after controlling for the average G10 slope, the U.S.
yield curve, and other standard measures of global risk.
The paper also shows why elevated downside risk does not need imply that the carry
position should always be unwound. Inversions may reflect expected weakness in economic
activity or high policy rates set to fight inflation and expected to come down. The shape of
the average G10 curve helps to distinguish these environments. When the curve is strictly
downward sloping, the IRDs are unusually wide and, on average, compensate for most of
the subsequent depreciation.
Unwinding in the downcurve regime is therefore unusually
expensive, and an investor may prefer to overwrite the signal. The EM extension reinforces
this trade-off. The EM portfolios also depreciate on the signal, the beta-sorted one more than
its G10 counterpart, but their wide IRDs keep the EM carry trade’s total return positive.
Lastly, the paper translates the information in international yield curves into a monthly
conditioning variable for portfolio risk management. Much of the existing literature evalu-
ates exchange-rate predictability over horizons of several months or quarters, which do not
map directly into an investor’s current position. Freshness determines whether an inversion
remains informative, clustering identifies whether its content is common across countries,
and the shape of the curve distinguishes compensated from uncompensated currency risk.

Raising the threshold to three weakens the results, and the row below it shows why, since

would have chosen exactly two, which is the largest cell in all four columns; the nested ≥2
rule was chosen on economic grounds, and it is the one an investor can implement, because
an exact-count state switches off in the month a third curve inverts.

Timing.
Every regressor is observed at the end of month t and predicts the return of month
t + 1. A truncation audit rebuilds the signal from data ending at each of 435 month-ends
and finds that no month’s classification changes when later data arrives, and a second audit
finds every conditioning series dated T identical to its full-sample value. Every conditioning
variable is market-priced at month-end except the NFCI, and lagging the signal one additional
month decays the coefficients as an ex-ante state variable should.

The rival to the expectations interpretation is a common global term premium, proxied
by the U.S. ACM slope premium of Adrian et al. (2013). Entering it as a control, in level
and in changes, makes no difference to the headline. Table B.17 reports the more demanding
version, which strips each country’s slope of its fitted exposure and rebuilds the rule on the
stripped slopes. Of the 1,122 inverted curve-months it leaves 937 inverted, ends 185, and
creates 110 elsewhere. The rebuilt state is nonetheless wider, 124 months against 93, because
the rule counts inversions that arrive together and not how many there are. On that broader
state the beta HML portfolio loses much as on the baseline, −8.00%/yr in spot and −8.39
in total, both significant. The carry portfolio keeps about a third of its baseline loss and no
longer clears significance. The regime split survives in shape, though the downcurve gap is
no longer significant.
