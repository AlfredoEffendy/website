When Yield Curves Invert Together:
Currency Crash Risk∗
Alfredo Effendy†
University of Washington

Abstract
A yield-curve inversion is a well-documented leading indicator of domestic slowdown,
but a single inversion does not reveal whether the expected slowdown is country-specific
or global. This distinction, however, decisively predicts the asset market response to
the impending slowdown. In a portfolio of currencies, such as the carry trade, currency-
specific risk might be diversified, while systematic risk is affecting every risky currency.
I show that a signal constructed from clustered inversions predicts crashes in high-
interest and high-beta currencies, and the carry trade. This result plays a role in giving
an early warning to avoid the well-known carry trade crashes. Further conditioning
on the slope shape of global yield curves refines the signal to distinguish between
inversions driven by slowing growth expectations versus inflation-fighting monetary
policies. The latter comes with a higher interest rates environment, which provides a
higher compensation for the carry trade crash risk.

1 Introduction
The currency carry trade borrows in low-interest-rate currencies and invests in high-interest-
rate ones. It has been profitable on average because high-interest-rate currencies do not
depreciate enough to offset the interest rate differential (IRD), in violation of uncovered
interest parity (UIP) (Hansen and Hodrick, 1980; Fama, 1984; Engel, 2016). The modern
literature interprets this premium as compensation for depreciation risk and organizes cur-
rency returns into priced factors (Lustig and Verdelhan, 2007; Lustig et al., 2011; Menkhoff
et al., 2012). The premium accumulates gradually, but much of it can disappear during
currency crashes arising from unwinds of carry trade positions and funding spirals. These
crashes create negative return skewness (Brunnermeier et al., 2008), the carry trade has
exhibited across various financial market and monetary regimes in the past century (Ac-
cominotti et al., 2019). In disaster models, these losses are the realizations for which the
interest rate differential compensates investors in advance (Farhi and Gabaix, 2016; Chernov
et al., 2018). For a carry investor, the question that matters is whether an impending crash
is predictable.
Answering that question requires an indicator of when currency downside risk is elevated.
Government yield curves encode market expectations about economic activity in real time,
and an inversion has long been understood as a recession signal (Harvey, 1988; Estrella
and Hardouvelis, 1991; Campbell and Shiller, 1991; Estrella and Mishkin, 1998; Bauer and
Mertens, 2018). I find that a signal constructed from cross-country clusters of fresh yield
curve inversion predict currency crashes. Although the signal is only active in about a
fifth of the sample, it predicts four of the give worst months in carry trade. The losses
are concentrated in high-interest-rate and high-beta currencies. Figure 1 illustrates the
relationship between the signal and carry trade crashes.
This finding suggests that the bond market leads the currency market, with yield curve
inversions providing an early warning of currency losses. Two features of the signal help

simplest way to detect a cluster is to count how many curves are inverted in a given month,

There are several ways to account for freshness. This paper focuses on an approach
that uses the information from the yield curve itself. A curve becomes active when it first
inverts and remains active until its slope has steepened for two consecutive months, whether
or not the slope has returned above zero. Steepening indicates that either a recovery is
underway or the market has begun to price in the inversion, while the two-month confirmation
follows the two-quarter recession conventions used in the United Kingdom, Canada, and
Australia. Requiring the confirmation also handles curves whose slope hovers around zero,
which might otherwise create a noisy alternating inversion state. An alternative approach
is to penalize older clusters by weighting the naive count by the inverse of the cluster’s age,

I construct the IYC signal, from the G10 government yield curves, with both distinctions
above built in. The baseline configuration for the yield curve slope is the ten-year yield
minus the two-year yield, and Appendix B.1 repeats the construction with the ten-year
minus the three-month yield. I use a simple threshold of two active inversions to separate
a country-specific inversion from a cluster, since two is the smallest count that rules out
a single-country event. The signal is on in 93 of the 458 sample months (∼20%), across
ten different episodes. Figure 1 shows how the freshness consideration separates the IYC
signal from the naive count, which stays active for more months, and Section 2 gives the
full construction of the signal.

economies that are exposed to the global cycle (Ready et al., 2017; Della Corte et al., 2016).1

The carry trade inherits this exposure because it is long high-interest-rate, high-beta curren-
cies and short low-interest-rate, low-beta currencies. When the signal is active, a portfolio
that is long high-beta currencies and short low-beta currencies (beta HML) loses 9.24%

including IRD) against a gain of 2.64% (6.19% including IRD). By contrast, a currency
whose own curve is active while the signal is off does not depreciate on average, and months
with exactly one active inversion remain favorable for the carry trade, returning 10.09%
annualized.
The signal changes more than the average return. During these signal months, portfolio
skewness becomes more negative and returns deteriorate throughout the distribution, with
the largest changes in the left tail, accounting for much of the negative skewness of the carry
trade. Composite leading indicators (CLI) also decline across the G10 economies after the
signal months, including in the United States. Both the exchange rate and macroeconomic
results show that the depreciation in high-interest-rate high-beta currencies and declining
are due to the bond market pricing in a systematic weakening in economic activities, rather
than one country domestic slowdown. In the language of the disaster-risk literature (Rietz,
1988; Barro, 2006; Wachter, 2013), I interpret the IYC signal as an observable indicator of
elevated currency downside risk.
An inversion can occur in two different environments. First, market participants may
expect rates to fall because of weakening economic activity (growth-scare); second, central
banks may have or be in the process of raising rates to fight inflation, which market partici-
pants may read as transitory. A growth-scare inversion usually occurs in a low-interest-rate
environment that generates a low compensation to potential depreciation in the carry trade.
An inflation-fighting inversion occurs in a high interest rate environment, providing a large
compensation for the carry trade. In the latter case, the IRD compensates the trader enough
to offset the risk of a crash.
4
The shape of the yield curve helps to distinguish these cases. When the three-month
yield is higher than the two-year yield and the two-year yield is higher than the ten-year
yield, it represents an environment where the short-term rate is the highest and the market
is expecting the interest rate to go down systematically in the medium and long term. I call
this strictly downward-sloping configuration the downcurve and use the average G10 yield
curve to measure it. In the months with this downcurve shape, the IRD compensates most
of the depreciation that follows, while in other cases, it only compensates a small fraction of
the depreciation. One can think of the signal as an early warning of an elevated crash risk,
while the downcurve indicates whether the risk is adequately compensated.
A follow-up question is how to apply the constructed signal in real-time portfolio risk
management. The natural response is to unwind the position when the signal is active to
hedge the upcoming crashes. Doing so however, forgoes the IRD, which in months with
downcurve configuration, compensates most of the losses. I call the risk of surrendering this
compensation carry-premium risk, and the decision to go against the signal to stay invested
the carry overwrite. Other options that investors and traders can do are hedging the tail
via options Jurek (2014) or going anti-carry (Caballero and Doyle, 2012) during these signal
months.
One potential concern is that the signal only repackages the global yield curve, the U.S.
yield curve, or standard risk and volatility predictors such as the VIX, FX volatility, commod-
ity index, and the NFCI. I therefore run a horse race regression against all those measures,
including each country’s inversion, the U.S. included, and the results’ power survives or even
strengthens in some cases.
The signal is also distinct from the curve-based predictors existing in the currency litera-
ture. The closest yield-curve studies use relative Nelson-Siegel factors, curvature, and other
shape measures to predict bilateral exchange rates and currency returns Chen and Tsang
5
(2013); Dreher et al. (2020); Lloyd and Marin (2024); Lustig et al. (2019). 2 These measure
the difference between two countries’ curves and remove the component they share, which is
what we are interested in detecting. A pairwise result against the dollar also does not map
directly into a diversified currency portfolio.
Conditioning carry trade performance on observable states is not new. Clarida et al.
(2009) and Christiansen et al. (2011) allow the carry trade’s returns and risk exposures
to vary in different volatility and liquidity regimes, while Barroso and Santa-Clara (2015)
construct currency portfolios from observable covariates. Other related works condition
on average interest rate levels (Lustig et al., 2014), inflation expectations (Andrews et al.,
2024), and commodity, volatility, and liquidity predictors (Bakshi and Panayotov, 2013).
The novel element is the conditioning variable, where a fresh cross-country cluster of yield-
curve inversions is used to identify an elevated crash risk in the currency market, and I am
not aware of any prior work that uses this specific approach.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 presents the measure of risk
compensation, and the portfolio and signal construction. Section 3 reports the predictive
evidence, the cross-sectional exposure mechanism, the change in the return distribution, and
the portfolio hedging application. Section 4 compares the signal with other curve measures
and standard risk indicators. Section 5 extends the study to emerging market currencies.

2.5 Data
The main sample uses monthly data and runs from January 1988 to February 2026. The
baseline universe is the nine G10 currencies (AUD, NZD, CAD, NOK, SEK, GBP, EUR,
CHF, and JPY), and I use the DEM as a proxy for the EUR before 1999. For each currency,
I use its exchange rate against the dollar, its one-month money-market rate, and its 3M,
2Y, and 10Y government bond yields. The currency betas are estimated on daily (monthly)
exchange rate and S&P 500 (MSCI World) returns, both of which are available 90 trading
days (24 months) before the start of the sample.
For the EM extension, I add the seven most liquid floating currencies, BRL, CLP, IDR,
INR, MXN, KRW, and ZAR. Due to data availability, their sample is shorter. The last
money-market rate to start is the Chilean Peso, which started in January 1996. So, the EM
panel begins in February 1996, while the alternative signal built on the EM curves themselves
begins in June 2003, the first month when the EM curves are available.
Among the other variables, the NFCI, the CLI, and CPI inflation are available from
January 1988, while the VIX begins in October 1990. The CLI ends in November 2019 for
New Zealand and in November 2022 for Switzerland, Norway, and Sweden, and I extend
each with that country’s business or consumer confidence index, which affects the CLI result
in some of the IYC signal months. All data are obtained from Bloomberg, Finaeon, central
bank datasets, and the Chicago Fed. Appendix A sets out the economic regimes the sample
spans.

3 IYC Signal Mechanism and Implementation
A synchronized yield curve inversion indicates an expected slowdown in different economies.
We can think of it as a systematic risk building in the currency market; therefore, curren-
cies that are exposed more to this risk (measured by the currency beta) are expected to
depreciate more. The carry trade sorts the currency based on the IRD, which has a high
correlation with the currency beta, which the beta HML portfolio sorts with. As a result,
these portfolios are exposed to the systematic risk signaled by the IYC. This section shows
this mechanism on the currency level through the IRD and currency beta ordering for the
IYC signal (Section 3.2), then shows it in a portfolio setting (Section 3.1). Additionally, it
also provides Fama regression conditioning on the IYC signal (Section 3.3), portfolio distri-
bution conditioning on the IYC signal (Section 3.4), and the trading application of the IYC
signal on the carry trade (Section 3.5).

There are several properties of the data and signals that we need to consider when
choosing the statistical methodology. First, monthly return errors are serially correlated and
heteroskedastic, a common issue when dealing with financial time series data. Second, the
signals are persistent; following the property of an inversion, once a yield curve is inverted,
it tends to be inverted for several months. Third, inversions occur relatively infrequently, so
21
we are dealing with a rare event study. As a result, the constructed signal creates a block
of signals across different time periods; we may have many signal months, but the effective
number of periods is significantly smaller than that. The baseline IYC signal is active in 93
months, but the effective number of independent episodes is only between 5 to 14 (depending
on the definition of an episode, whether it is the number of clusters, imposing a minimum
gap between episodes, or based on the business cycle).
I provide two complementary p-values to address these concerns. The first one is using
the wild cluster bootstrap (WCB) with Rademacher weights and imposing the null hypothe-
sis (Cameron et al., 2008). This method holds the signal fixed and applies the same random
weight, either (+1) or (-1), to the restricted residuals within each cluster. All the months
belonging to the same episode receive the same weight, and it allows the errors to be cor-
related within the clusters, while assuming independence across clusters. I use replication
B = 9,999 times following Davidson and MacKinnon (2000) for precision purposes. The
second is the Newey and West (1987) p-value with 12-month lags, in order to capture the
persistency of the signal, which accounts for the heteroskedasticity and serial correlation
through the selected window.

3.2 Interest Rate Differentials and Currency Betas
Crashes in the carry trade happen when the high-interest rate currencies depreciate signifi-
cantly more relative to the low-interest rate currencies. When a currency is offering a high
interest rate, it compensates for the risk that it bears, including exposure to global growth.
24
This channel relates the currency IRD with the currency beta, and in Table 4, it is shown
that the two are highly correlated across the sample (ρ= 0.87).
Connecting the IRD and beta to the IYC signals shows how they perform across different
currencies. The currencies with higher beta and IRD, such as AUD, NZD, and CAD depreci-
ate significantly more compared to the safe haven currencies (Ranaldo and S¨oderlind, 2010)
with lower beta and IRD, such as the JPY, CHF, and EUR. Especially with the downcurve
filter, the regression coefficients of these individual currencies goes from extremely negative
for the AUD to positive for the JPY. The carry trade and beta HML portfolio long the
high-beta high-IRD currencies while short the low-beta low-IRD currencies, which explains
why those portfolios crash during the IYC signals. Figure 6 shows the relationship between
the currency beta and IRD, and their performance during the signal months.

3.3 Fama Regressions
The results in Section 3.2 suggest an implication for the forward premium puzzle. There
is a significant depreciation for currencies when the IYC is active. So, the question here
is whether it can explain help to explain the puzzle by providing a period where it mean
reverts. I estimate the Fama (1984) regression with the signals in order to study this. The
first equation is the original regression, while the second one introduces the signal in the
regression:

If the UIP holds, the regression coefficient for (15) (γF) must be equal to -1. However, it
is well documented in the literature and also in Table 6 that most of the currencies instead
have a positive γF, with the pooled coefficient across G10 currencies equal to +0.49. After
introducing the signals into the equation, the distinction is clear. We can interpret the
result as a slow consistent deviation from the UIP (”staircase up”) in the months that are
downcurve IYC months, and a violent reversion (”elevator down”) for the UIP during the
non-downcurve IYC months in the terminology of Brunnermeier et al. (2008). This result
also complements other studies on the puzzle (Verdelhan, 2010; Farhi and Gabaix, 2016;
Chernov et al., 2018) by introducing a state variable for the mean reversion periods.

downcurve overwrite (staying in even though we observe the IYC signal). Figure 7 shows

4.1 Individual, United States, and Global Yield Curves
The United States yield curve is often used as a proxy for a global risk in the literature, which
makes sense since the United States is the biggest economy in the world, and if something is
happening there, it should have an impact to the rest of the world. Therefore, it is natural
to question whether the IYC signal adds information beyond the United States yield curve.
This is also the reason why I excluded the United States yield curve when constructing the
baseline IYC signal. An alternative construction including the United States and the dollar
does not change the result (Table B.5).
The IYC signal is constructed from the aggregation of the G10 yield curves. So, it is
important to test it against the aggregate G10 yield slope to show that it adds information
beyond the aggregate slope. Table 9 presents the horse race against the United States
inversion, level and change of the 10Y-2Y slope, the level and change of the G10 10Y-
2Y slope. The result is robust controlling all of these yield curve variables with none of
those variables are statistically meaningful, which confirms that the IYC signal add more
information than the United States and aggregate G10 yield curves.

6 Conclusion
This paper contributes to the yield curve and the international asset pricing literature by
showing that the IYC signal constructed from clusters of fresh G10 yield curve inversions
identify periods of elevated systematic crash risk for the currency market. The freshness of
the inversion is important to distinguish information that still matters for the asset market
and those that have gone stale; while the clustering helps to isolate systematic risk from
idiosyncratic risk. The crashes live in high-beta and high-interest-rate currencies, which
helps to predict the carry trade crashes. The signal covers about 20% of the sample, but is
able to capture four of the five worst months in the carry trade. The result is robust upon
controlling other global risk factors such as the VIX, FX Volatility, NFCI, and the United
States yield curves and dollar factor.
This paper also provides an approach to distinguish different environment of the inver-
sions, namely the growth-scare inversion and inflation-fighting inversion, by looking if the
average G10 yield curve is strictly downward sloping (”downcurve). Inversions accompanied
by this downcurve shape come during periods of high inflation, providing higher IRD for
the carry trade, compensating most of the crash risk. An analogy to this can be seen when
extending the study to EM currencies in general, where their spot is depreciating, but their
40
IRD is very high that it still provides a positive carry return. This result gives a perspective
that some risks are compensated that it is worth bearing them.
Lastly, much of the existing literature evaluates the currency predictability over several
horizons, which does not map directly into investors’ portfolio decision. This paper con-
tributes to the risk management literature by providing an actionable signal that investors
can use in real-time to manage their currency portfolios. Investors can unwind partially or
fully, go anti-carry, or hedge using options when the IYC signal is active. Moreover, when
they also observe the downcurve shape, the can overwrite the signal and choose to stay
invested because the risk that they are bearing compensated by the environment.
